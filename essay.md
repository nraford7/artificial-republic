# Build an AI Republic, Not a Prince

### What Venice's institutional pressures can teach us about building agentic AI systems

I am writing this from Ca' da Mosto, a merchant house on the Grand Canal in Venice. Across the water is the Fabbriche Nuove, Sansovino's long arcade beside the Rialto, built in 1555. It is not a palace. There is no princely entrance. Just identical bays, one after the next: office, office, office. The building housed the administrative organs of a commercial state that was, by the time it got this facade, already three hundred years old.

Venice developed specialised magistracies, written procedures, commercial courts, financial auditing, public debt instruments, diplomatic intelligence, maritime regulation, quarantine, and elaborate checks on its own officeholders, centuries before most European states needed anything of the kind. Not because Venetians were unusually wise, but because they were unusually pressured.

A normal medieval kingdom was built around land. Authority, military power, and wealth were bundled together: king, great nobles, lesser nobles, peasants. Venice had very little of that. Its ruling families became rich through trade, ships, finance, manufacturing, and overseas concessions. That produces different organisational problems. A merchant sending a ship to Alexandria needs enforceable contracts, standardised weights, insurance-like arrangements, credit, courts, diplomatic protection, and some confidence that a rival cannot simply bribe the government to ruin him. Twenty investors might hold interests in cargo aboard a single ship, partly financed with borrowed money, carrying goods owned by different merchants, under a captain with contractual obligations, into foreign jurisdictions with their own treaties, customs, and laws, and the whole thing takes months.

You very quickly need records, contracts, notaries, courts, standardised procedures, specialised officials, and auditing. The men writing those rules were not an external bureaucracy imposed on the merchants. They *were* the merchants, with their own fortunes at stake. Their private wealth depended on the quality of their public institutions. That alignment is the key to why it worked.

The result was something that looked less like a feudal society and more like an early corporate state: narrow mandates, rotation of offices, mandatory audits, overlapping oversight, anti-capture mechanisms, and institutional memory that outlived any individual officeholder. Several hundred patrician families effectively became shareholders in the Republic after the Serrata of 1297 closed the Great Council. No single family was supposed to own the state. But collectively, the patriciate did, and they had an enormous shared incentive to prevent any one of themselves from becoming king. That produced rules, committees, term limits, multiple approvals, and sometimes absurd bureaucracy. It also made personal dictatorship nearly impossible for a thousand years.

AI agents are beginning to restructure how organisations work: how decisions get made, how tasks get delegated, how information flows, who or what holds authority. We are standing up systems of autonomous agents that can read documents, write code, move money, contact customers, and make consequential choices. Mostly, we are doing it by handing one powerful model the keys and hoping it behaves. That is the design Venice spent centuries learning to distrust.

The pressures are not identical, but they rhyme in specific ways. Both involve capable autonomous actors operating across jurisdictions and time horizons that no single mind can hold. Both face the problem of coordinating parties that do not share an objective. Both need institutional memory that survives the departure of any individual. And both must decide how much friction to accept in exchange for how much safety. The Venetian experience offers several concrete lessons.

**No single agent should investigate, decide, execute, and verify its own work.** Venice separated these functions across magistracies on purpose. One body gathered evidence, another weighed it, another authorised the penalty, another recorded the outcome. The agent that tells you it transferred fifty thousand euros should not be the sole authority confirming the transfer succeeded.

**Narrow mandates beat general-purpose power.** Venice did not build a Ministry of Everything. Shipping got complicated, so it created maritime magistrates. Grain became strategic, grain officials. Plague arrived, a board of health. Each office had its own tools, permissions, and definition of success. The finance magistrate had no authority over the harbourmaster. In agentic terms: a research agent has no business holding payment credentials; a procurement agent has no reason to access the codebase.

**Offices should rotate; memory should not.** Venetian officials served short terms and moved on. Institutional competence survived because the state was obsessive about writing everything down: decisions, precedents, dispatches, ledgers. The office persisted; the officeholder was replaceable. An AI agent's context window is ephemeral by nature, brilliant for thirty seconds, then gone. The lesson is not just "persist state" but "design for turnover." The system must assume any individual agent will be replaced at any time, and nothing critical should live only inside a single context.

**Randomise selection to break collusion.** The Venetian ducal election alternated lot and ballot through multiple rounds until forty-one final electors emerged. The whole machine existed to make it impossible for any faction to pre-arrange the outcome. In multi-agent orchestration, if you always route a task to the same agent or select the same verifier, you create capture risk. Injecting randomness into agent assignment is, as far as I know, largely unexplored. It should not be.

**Redundancy only works if the redundant systems are genuinely independent.** Venice built overlapping jurisdictions, several magistracies able to notice the same wrongdoing. That works because human magistrates had independent judgment, different political incentives, and different information. Two instances of the same language model reviewing each other's output share training data, failure modes, and blind spots. That is correlated failure masquerading as independence. Genuine redundancy requires a different model, a formal invariant check, or a human in the loop.

**The supervisor needs constraints too.** Venice created the Council of Ten to watch the other magistracies. But it did not leave the question "who watches the watcher?" unanswered. The Ten served one-year terms, could not be immediately reappointed, and were checked by the Capi and the Avogadori di Comun. The lesson for AI: the monitoring agent should have a finite scope, a limited lifespan, and its own actions should be logged by a separate, minimal process it cannot influence.

**Alignment through environment, not through values.** The Republic never pretended its patricians shared a single goal. The Corner family wanted what the Contarini did not. Merchants competed, factions formed, everyone chased money and rank. Venice's answer was not to make its citizens virtuous. It was to design the environment, rotation, audits, transparency, randomised selection, overlapping oversight, so that bad behaviour was caught whether the individual intended it or not. Francis Fukuyama, across *The Origins of Political Order* and *Political Order and Political Decay*, argues that state capacity, the ability to build institutions that constrain the powerful, matters more than resources or talent. Venice is his thesis built in stone. The equivalent challenge in agentic AI is not making the model behave well. It is designing the system so the system holds even when the model does not.

I spent a decade as a futurist advising a government that was, in effect, trying to build institutions at speed, and I watched what happens when you get the plumbing wrong. The buildings are magnificent. The decisions are terrible. The Venetians understood something we keep forgetting: you do not make a complex system reliable by finding one brilliant decision-maker. You make it reliable by decomposing authority, specialising functions, recording state, and forcing consequential actions through checks. That is governance. It is the hardest kind of intelligence to build, and we have barely started.

Venice lasted a thousand years on that principle. It also eventually collapsed, ossified by the very oligarchy that once made it strong. Both halves of that sentence matter.
