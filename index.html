What Venetian governance can teach us about designing AI for complex, messy systems.

By Noah Raford  ·  Written on the Grand Canal, Venice

Scroll

Systems respond to their environment. Venice — a republic whose wealth sailed on vessels it could not supervise — built one of the most sophisticated governance architectures in history to survive that fact.

Its solutions were structural: narrow mandates, overlapping jurisdictions, persistent memory, constitutional constraints, and a head of state deliberately hollowed of real power.

These mechanisms map, dimension by dimension, onto the design problems of multi-agent AI systems — distributed autonomous actors, incomplete information, competing interests, stakes too high for casual governance.

The question Venice answered, and the one AI development has mostly failed to ask, is not “how intelligent can we make the agent?” but “how intelligent can we make the system?”

The structural parallel to agentic AI is not metaphorical. The constraints Venice faced — distributed autonomous actors, incomplete information, competing interests, high stakes, trust across distance without direct supervision — are the constraints of multi-agent system design. Venice’s solutions map onto eight specific dimensions:

  • Data quality — provenance and cross-referencing, not unchecked inputs

  • Rules and constraints — constitutional permissions, not prompt-level suggestions

I

What Venice Built

Land vs. seaA feudal lord’s wealth sat still. Venice’s wealth moved across oceans. The sea forced institutional invention the way it forced better hulls.

Venice had almost no land. Its wealth came through the water — through ventures that could sink, literally, on any given voyage. A typical trading expedition around 1250 involved twenty investors pooling capital, borrowed money leveraged against future cargo, a captain bound by contractual obligations specifying which ports to visit, customs duties varying by treaty, insurance-like arrangements spreading risk across dozens of parties. Everything took months. Nothing could be supervised directly. And fortunes hung on the outcome.

This environment produced institutional invention at a pace and depth that Europe’s land-based monarchies could not match. A feudal lord collected rents from tenants bound to the soil whether the bureaucracy was sharp or dull. Venice’s merchants needed enforceable contracts, standardized weights, courts that moved faster than the sailing season, diplomatic protection in foreign ports, and confidence that a rival could not bribe the government to destroy them. The men writing the rules were the men whose fortunes sailed under them. Private wealth depended on institutional quality.

What emerged was governance built on a principle most modern organizations still resist: institutional distrust as a design philosophy. Everyone has interests. The system’s job is to arrange institutions so that those interests constrain one another.

What This Tells Us

The dominant question in AI development today is “how intelligent can we make the agent?” — better reasoning, larger context windows, more capable individual models. This is the Doge question: how brilliant can we make the single actor at the center? Venice spent three centuries learning that this is the wrong question. The gaps are real. Venice operated on human timescales — deliberation took weeks, institutional evolution took generations. Agent systems operate at machine speed, and the failure modes of distributed governance at millisecond timescales are genuinely unknown. And the Venetian system, for all its sophistication, eventually calcified into the inability to adapt. These are not reasons to dismiss the comparison. They are reasons to take it seriously as a starting point rather than a finished answer. The Fabbriche Nuove does not tell us what to build. It tells us what question to ask. There is a building in Venice that can teach us more about designing AI systems than any whitepaper published this year. It sits on the Grand Canal at the Rialto bend, a long, low, repetitive facade of arched windows running above a commercial arcade. The Fabbriche Nuove di Rialto, designed by Jacopo Sansovino and completed in 1559, is not beautiful in the way Venice is supposed to be beautiful. No marble filigree, no gilded saints, no Byzantine theater. Its rhythm is bureaucratic: window, column, window, column, window, an unbroken horizontal that whispers parsimonia e dell’utile — thrift and utility. The same Sansovino designed the lavish Biblioteca Marciana at San Marco, where the message was “We are a magnificent imperial republic.” At the Rialto, the message was different. We run an extremely serious business.

From across the canal, sitting in what was once the Ca’ da Mosto — a merchant’s palazzo from the thirteenth century — you can read the Fabbriche Nuove not as architecture but as argument. The building exists because specific environmental pressures demanded it. Venice’s ruling families grew rich through trade, ships, finance, and overseas concessions, not through feudal rents collected from peasants who had no choice but to pay.

A merchant sending a galley to Alexandria needed enforceable contracts, standardized weights, insurance arrangements, credit instruments, courts that moved faster than the sailing season, diplomatic protection in foreign ports, and confidence that a rival could not bribe the government to destroy him. The men writing the rules were not an external bureaucracy. They were merchants regulating a system in which they themselves had fortunes at risk. Private wealth depended on public institutional quality, and institutional quality depended on getting governance right in conditions of radical uncertainty.

That building across the canal is the physical residue of their solution: not a single brilliant ruler, but a distributed system of narrow, overlapping, mutually suspicious institutions. It housed magistracies supervising commerce and mercantile disputes, each with its own mandate, tools, and limits. Ground floor: shops, warehouses, commercial infrastructure. Upper floors: the machinery of governance. Form followed function, and the function was managing complexity that no individual — however wise — could hold in a single mind.

This is the thesis. Systems respond to their environment. Their institutional architectures are shaped by the pressures, constraints, and needs they must survive. And the pressures Venice faced between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries forced one of the most sophisticated governance architectures in human history — centuries before modern management theory, corporate governance frameworks, or systems thinking had names.

I

What Venice Built

To understand what made Venice different, start with what made it exposed.

A land-based kingdom could afford mediocre institutions. A feudal lord collected rents from tenants bound to the soil; the revenue came whether the bureaucracy was sharp or dull, whether the courts were fast or glacial. Territorial control was the asset, and territorial control could be maintained through force. Venice had almost no land. Its wealth came through the water — through ventures that could sink, literally, on any given voyage.

Consider a typical Venetian trading expedition around 1250: twenty investors pooling capital, borrowed money leveraged against future cargo, different merchants owning different portions of the hold, a captain bound by contractual obligations specifying which ports to visit, the Republic itself prohibiting certain cargoes for diplomatic reasons, customs duties varying by treaty, foreign sovereigns whose goodwill could evaporate between one sailing season and the next, liability for shipwreck apportioned by contract, insurance-like arrangements spreading risk across dozens of parties. Everything took months. Nothing could be supervised directly. And fortunes — personal, familial, sometimes national — hung on the outcome.

The 1250 voyage20 investors, borrowed money, multiple cargo owners, a captain under contract, customs at both ends, foreign treaties, shipwreck liability, piracy claims. No real-time communication. The institutional apparatus to manage this predated modern corporate governance by 500 years.

This environment, sustained across centuries of Mediterranean trade, produced institutional invention at a pace and depth that Europe’s land-based monarchies could not match. The demands were relentless: records demanded contracts, contracts demanded notaries, notaries demanded courts, courts demanded standardized procedures, procedures demanded specialized officials, officials demanded auditing. Each link in the chain existed because the previous one was insufficient.

What emerged was remarkable. Venice developed a governance architecture built on a principle that most modern organizations still resist: institutional distrust as a design philosophy. Everyone has interests. The system’s job is to arrange institutions so that those interests constrain one another.

Institutional distrustNot cynicism — design. The assumption that every actor has interests, and that institutions should be arranged so those interests constrain one another.

The Doge, Venice’s head of state, illustrates the principle at its most extreme. Over generations, the Venetians hollowed out the office. The Doge was magnificent — crowned, robed, ceremonially supreme — and almost powerless. His election required an alternating sequence of lottery and vote so baroque it took multiple rounds to complete, specifically designed to prevent any faction from capturing the outcome.

The Hollowed DogeCould not open mail without witnesses. Could not conduct foreign policy alone. Could not name a successor. Could not leave Venice. The most visible leader was, by design, the least powerful.

He was, in modern terms, a constitutional figurehead engineered to prevent exactly the failure mode that destroyed most of Venice’s rivals: the concentration of authority in a single brilliant, corruptible, mortal individual.

Francis FukuyamaPolitical scientist. In The Origins of Political Order, argues that state capacity — the ability to build institutions that constrain the powerful — matters more than natural resources. Venice is his thesis rendered in stone and water.

Below the Doge, Venice built an extraordinary network of narrow magistracies — separate bodies governing taxation, grain supply, salt, maritime regulation, shipbuilding, foreign trade, market supervision, sanitation, canals, customs, public health, espionage, state security. Each operated within a precisely defined mandate, its own tools, its own permissions. No single magistracy could reach beyond its boundaries. As the political scientist Francis Fukuyama has argued in The Origins of Political Order, Venice’s state capacity was unusual precisely because it distributed authority across institutions rather than concentrating it.

Persistent information systems completed the architecture: written reporting, diplomatic dispatches, registries, archives. Venetian ambassadors produced relazioni — end-of-mission reports synthesizing intelligence from foreign courts — that were archived, cross-referenced, and consulted by subsequent officials. Institutional memory outlived individual officers.

Overlapping jurisdictions created redundancy as a security mechanism. Multiple bodies had the authority to notice the same misconduct. Any significant action moved through a sequence of proposal, challenge, authorization, execution, and audit, with different bodies responsible at each stage. No single institution served as detective, prosecutor, judge, executioner, and accountant simultaneously.

Council of TenCreated after the Bajamonte Tiepolo coup attempt of 1310. Extraordinary powers: surveillance, investigation, suspension of officials. The supervisory agent — necessary and dangerous. Venice spent centuries fighting over its proper scope.

And the Council of Ten, established after the Bajamonte Tiepolo conspiracy of 1310, wielded extraordinary powers to monitor, detect anomalies, investigate, and escalate — the watchers who watched the watchers, though Venice understood that this merely displaced the problem one level up rather than solving it.

Serrata, 1297Restricted the Great Council to ~200 hereditary families. The effect: a permanent “shareholder class” whose private wealth depended on institutional quality. Mechanism design before the term existed.

And then there was the Serrata of 1297, which restricted membership in the Great Council to a hereditary patriciate of several hundred families. This is typically read as oligarchic closure. It was also something else: it made those families shareholders in the Republic, their personal fortunes inseparable from the institutional quality of the state. The men writing the rules were the men whose fortunes sailed under them. Incentive alignment enforced through the architecture of governance itself.

The Venetian system emerged from a specific set of environmental pressures:

Read that list again, slowly, and replace “merchants in distant ports” with “agents running autonomously across distributed systems.” Replace “the Doge” with “a single orchestrating model with root access.” Replace “relazioni from foreign courts” with “shared context across an agent chain.” Replace “overlapping jurisdictions” with “adversarial evaluation and redundant verification.”

The constraints Venice faced across centuries of maritime commerce map, point for point, onto the constraints of designing multi-agent AI systems today. And the Venetian solutions — arrived at through hard experience, institutional failure, conspiracy, corruption, and iterative correction over hundreds of years — contain design principles that the AI field is only beginning to articulate from first principles.

The Venice material was never historical tourism. It is direct precedent.

III

Eight Dimensions

The comparison earns its weight only if it holds at the level of specific mechanisms. Here are eight dimensions where Venice’s governance architecture and agentic system design face the same structural problem — and where the Venetian solution illuminates the AI one.

RelazioniFormal end-of-mission reports from Venetian ambassadors. Standardized format, archived and cross-referenced across decades. Venice’s institutional memory system — and the ancestor of persistent shared state.

Shared context. Venice’s challenge: how do you maintain coherent state across a system where individual actors are separated by months of travel time and operating on local information? Their solution was architectural — the relazioni, the archival system, the registries that accumulated institutional memory independent of any individual. When a new ambassador arrived at a foreign court, he did not start from zero; he read his predecessors’ reports. The context was persistent, structured, and accessible.

Agentic systems face the identical problem in compressed timescale. Individual agents operate on local context windows, and when context is ephemeral — when the compute finishes and the state vanishes — the system loses its memory between operations. The Venetian lesson: durable, structured, shared state is not a feature. It is the foundation without which distributed intelligence degrades into distributed guessing.

Least privilegeGive each actor exactly the authority its function requires — no more. A 500-year-old security principle, tested through centuries of Venetian conspiracies and near-collapses.

Delegation of authority. Venice never granted a magistracy more mandate than a specific function required. The salt office governed salt. The grain office governed grain. Authority was narrow by design, and the narrowness was the safety mechanism.

In agent architectures, the equivalent is scoped mandates — an agent authorized to execute within a defined boundary, forbidden from reaching beyond it. The instinct to build a general-purpose agent that can “do anything” is the instinct to build a Doge: magnificent, flexible, and a single point of catastrophic failure. Venice learned that narrow mandates are not a limitation on capability. They are how capability survives contact with reality.

Layered councilsGreat Council: legislation. Senate: foreign policy. Council of Ten: security. Collegio: executive coordination. Magistracies: domain-specific operations. Not a hierarchy — a routing architecture.

Orchestration. Venice’s council system — the Great Council, the Senate, the Council of Ten, the various magistracies — was not a hierarchy in the modern corporate sense. It was an orchestration layer: different bodies activated for different classes of decision, with escalation paths, veto mechanisms, and lateral channels. Routine commerce moved through the magistracies. Diplomatic strategy moved through the Senate. Existential threats moved through the Council of Ten.

The orchestration was the intelligence. Agent systems require the same structural thinking: not a flat pool of equivalent agents, but an orchestration architecture that routes different classes of task to different capabilities, with clear escalation paths when a task exceeds an agent’s mandate or competence.

Paul ChristianoAI safety researcher. Argues that trustworthiness should be a structural property of the system, not a property of individual agents.

Trust and verification. The Venetians did not trust individuals. They did not design their system to produce trust. They designed it to function in the absence of trust, through overlapping jurisdictions that made any significant misconduct visible to multiple independent bodies. Redundancy was not inefficiency; it was the security model.

In agent systems, the equivalent is adversarial evaluation — independent verification agents that review outputs not because the primary agent is expected to fail, but because any single agent’s output is, by definition, unchecked until a second perspective examines it. As the AI safety researcher Paul Christiano has argued, the question is not whether you can trust the agent. The question is whether you have arranged the system so that trustworthiness is a structural property, not an individual one.

The Venetian architecture assumed corruption was inevitable and built the system to detect and contain it. The honest version of the same assumption in AI: hallucination, drift, and error are not bugs to be eliminated from individual agents. They are properties to be detected and contained by the system.

Data quality. Venice’s diplomatic intelligence network was, for its era, without peer. Ambassadors were trained observers, their reports followed structured formats, and the archival system allowed cross-referencing across decades and geographies. The Republic could triangulate intelligence from multiple sources, weigh competing accounts, and identify which informants had track records of accuracy.

Cynefin frameworkDavid Snowden’s model distinguishing complex systems (probe-sense-respond) from merely complicated ones (sense-analyze-respond). Venice governed a complex system. Most agentic architectures are designed as if the problem were merely complicated.

For agentic workflows, the parallel is data provenance — knowing where information came from, how it was produced, how reliable the producing agent has been historically, and whether multiple independent sources corroborate it. As David Snowden’s Cynefin framework reminds us, in complex environments the quality of your decisions cannot exceed the quality of your sense-making. An agent chain that cannot trace the provenance of its inputs is a Venetian Republic that accepts any traveler’s story as diplomatic intelligence.

Stuart RussellAuthor of Human Compatible. Argues that alignment is a property of the architecture, not the agent.

Goal alignment. The Serrata was, among other things, a mechanism design solution. By making the patriciate’s wealth inseparable from the Republic’s institutional quality, Venice aligned private incentives with public outcomes. A patrician who corrupted the system was, in a concrete financial sense, corrupting the platform on which his own fortune depended.

The alignment problem in AI — how do you ensure that an agent’s behavior serves the objectives it was designed to serve? — has the same shape. As Stuart Russell has argued in Human Compatible, alignment is a property of the architecture, not the agent. The Venetian answer suggests that alignment is not primarily a training problem or a prompting problem. It is a structural problem, solved through the architecture of incentives and constraints, not through better instructions to individual agents.

Rules and constraints. Venice governed through constitutional order — codified rules, established procedures, explicit prohibitions — not through the personal judgment of wise leaders. The rules were the system. Individual judgment operated within the rules, not above them.

Prompt vs. permissionA prompt that says “don’t access the database” is a suggestion. An architecture that withholds the credentials is a constraint. Venice understood the difference five centuries ago.

In agent architectures, the distinction between encoded permissions and prompt-based rules matters enormously. A prompt is a suggestion. An encoded permission is a constraint. Venice discovered, through bitter experience with the Bajamonte Tiepolo conspiracy and others, that systems governed by suggestions — “the Doge should not accumulate too much power” — fail precisely when the pressure is highest. Constitutional constraints — “the Doge cannot accumulate power because the architecture prevents it” — survive.

Authorization and permissions. The hollowed Doge is the clearest lesson in the entire Venetian system. Venice took its most powerful office and, over generations, removed its powers — not because the Doges were bad, but because the architecture of concentrated authority was dangerous regardless of the individual who held it. The principle of least privilege, arrived at through centuries of political evolution.

Root access = the DogeVenice did not arrive at least privilege through theory. It arrived there through watching what happened when it gave the Doge real power. The lesson came expensive. We have the option of learning it cheaper.

In agent systems, the equivalent question is stark: should any single agent have root access? The Venetian answer, purchased through real conspiracies and real blood, is unambiguous. The most dangerous architecture is the one where a single actor — however intelligent, however well-intentioned — can take actions that the rest of the system cannot review, override, or reverse.

IV

What This Tells Us

The Venice comparison does not produce a neat checklist. It produces a reframe.

The Doge Question“How capable can we make the single actor at the center?” Venice spent three centuries learning this is the wrong question. The right one: how intelligent can we make the system?

The dominant question in AI development today is “how intelligent can we make the agent?” Better reasoning, larger context windows, more sophisticated tool use, more capable individual models. This is the Doge question — how brilliant, how capable, how omniscient can we make the single actor at the center? Venice spent three centuries learning that this is the wrong question. The right question is: how intelligent can we make the system?

System intelligence is architectural. It lives in the relationships between components, not in the capability of any single component. It emerges from narrow mandates that prevent catastrophic overreach, overlapping jurisdictions that catch what individual actors miss, persistent memory that outlives any single operation, constitutional constraints that survive the moments when suggestions fail, and incentive structures that align individual agent behavior with system-level objectives.

Several concrete design principles follow. First, distrust is a feature, not a failure — design systems that function in the absence of trust between components, and you get systems that remain functional when any individual component degrades. Second, narrow mandates outperform broad ones in high-stakes environments, not because narrow agents are smarter, but because they are containable when they fail.

Third, memory must be architectural, not incidental — an agent system with ephemeral state is a republic that burns its archives after every session. Fourth, rules must be encoded in the architecture, not stated in the prompt. A prompt is a norm. A norm is exactly as strong as the pressure against it.

“The question is not how intelligent we can make the agent. The question is how intelligent we can make the system.
Venice fell in 1797To Napoleon’s army — not to a superior system. Institutional sophistication did not prevent all failure modes. The same rigidity that prevented capture eventually prevented adaptation.

The gaps are real, and honesty about them matters. Venice operated on human timescales — deliberation took weeks, correction took months, institutional evolution took generations. Agent systems operate at machine speed, and the failure modes of distributed governance at millisecond timescales are genuinely unknown.

Venice’s accountability structures ultimately rested on human judgment, social pressure, and the threat of exile or execution — mechanisms that have no obvious analogue for non-human agents. And the Venetian system, for all its sophistication, was not immune to decline; the very constitutional rigidity that protected the Republic eventually calcified into the inability to adapt, and Venice spent its final centuries as a magnificent museum of its own institutional achievements.

These are not reasons to dismiss the comparison. They are reasons to take it seriously as a starting point rather than a finished answer.

Stand at the Ca’ da Mosto and look across the canal. The Fabbriche Nuove is still there, its long repetitive facade still communicating the same message it communicated in 1559: that the hard problem of governance is not finding a brilliant leader but building a system that remains intelligent after any individual within it fails, defects, or dies. We are building autonomous systems that will operate at scales and speeds Venice’s patricians could not have imagined, making decisions with consequences they never had to contemplate. The Venetians had centuries to iterate toward their architecture. We have years, maybe less.

The building does not tell us what to build. It tells us what question to ask. And we are, for the most part, still asking the wrong one.

Noah Raford

Written from Ca’ da Mosto, on the Grand Canal, Venice.
